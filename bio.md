Can AI agents live in tomorrow’s city?

Miguel Ureña-Pliego is a PhD researcher in Transportation Systems Engineering at the Technical University of Munich (TUM) and a Research Affiliate at the MIT Media Lab, City Science. His research sits at the intersection of artificial intelligence, human behavior, transportation, and urban systems, exploring how we can better represent the complex decisions that shape how cities move and evolve.

His work starts from a fundamental challenge: we make major urban decisions using models that systematically misestimate transportation demand, while the projects they inform can take years to materialize due to permitting, institutional processes, and conflicts among stakeholders.

In his presentation, Miguel will explore how AI agents could experience possible future cities within city-scale digital twins, allowing us to explore and compare urban futures before they are built. Rather than modeling travel in isolation, these agents can represent the interconnected decisions that shape where people live, work, and travel, helping us understand how changes to housing, transportation, services, and the built environment might reshape the city.

This also opens the possibility of a different approach to zoning and permitting. Community-defined rules could be translated into models that developers use to test proposals before submission. Projects that already satisfy the agreed-upon criteria could move through permitting much faster, with much of the evaluation automated. As the digital twin evolves with new information and changing conditions, these models could enable a form of dynamic zoning: planning systems that adapt while remaining grounded in community-defined priorities.



Can AI agents live in tomorrow’s city?

Miguel Ureña-Pliego is a PhD researcher in Transportation Systems Engineering at the Technical University of Munich (TUM) and a Research Affiliate at the MIT Media Lab, City Science. His research sits at the intersection of artificial intelligence, human behavior, transportation, and urban systems, focusing on how we represent and understand the decisions that shape how cities move and evolve.

His work is motivated by a fundamental challenge: we make major decisions about cities using models that can systematically misestimate transportation demand, while the projects they inform can take years to materialize because of permitting, institutional processes, and conflicts among stakeholders.

A central concern in his research is how transportation models represent human behavior. Many models focus on relatively well-defined travel patterns, particularly journeys between home and work. But people’s decisions are interconnected: where we live and work is influenced by housing, family, schools, services, leisure, safety, and many other factors, which in turn shape how, when, and where we travel. Understanding mobility therefore requires understanding the broader system of decisions and activities that produces it.

Within the broader City Science approach, Miguel explores a two-way relationship between people and models.

Humans → Agents: How can information about real people and communities be transformed into realistic AI agents that operate within a city-scale digital twin? Which aspects of behavior are local and contextual, and which can be generalized across populations and cities?

Agents → Humans: How can those agents and digital twins help people understand and explore possible urban futures? Instead of using models only to predict what might happen, AI agents could experience simulated cities before they are built, allowing us to test thousands of alternative transportation systems, development strategies, and urban interventions and explore how people might respond to them.

This also enables a different approach to zoning and permitting. Community-defined rules and priorities could be translated into models that evaluate proposed developments before they enter the permitting process. Developers could test their proposals against these criteria in advance and identify potential conflicts before submitting them. If a proposal already satisfies the agreed-upon requirements, much of the evaluation could be automated, allowing projects to move through the permitting process much faster.

Over time, these models could incorporate new information and changing conditions, creating the possibility of dynamic zoning: planning and permitting systems that evolve while remaining grounded in rules and priorities established by the community. The broader idea is to move from models that simply forecast the city toward systems that let us experience, question, and explore the cities we have yet to build.
